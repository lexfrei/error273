# Design

What each planned layer will be built out of, and which shipped game each mechanic is taken from. The evidence is in `research/lifecycle.md`, `research/culture.md`, `research/zpg.md` and `research/worldgen.md`; every entry names the report section so the numbers can be checked against their sources. Layers 1-4 exist in `src/sim.rs`; everything from layer 5 on is a plan.

Four conventions run through the document. A line beginning `Open:` marks a choice the reports leave genuinely undecided, with both options stated; a line beginning `Conflicts with ADR NNNN:` marks a research proposal that contradicts a recorded decision. Each is followed by the call that was made — `Decision:` for an open choice, `Ruling:` for a conflict. The options and the conflicts are kept rather than edited away, because what was considered and rejected is part of the record.

## 1. Vision and constraints

A generator burns hauled wood on a frozen disc. Citizens carry needs, live in houses, are born, work, and die. The watcher has no input except a mayor, and the mayor is never obeyed unconditionally. The product is the simulation; the terminal is a window onto it.

The governing rule for the whole design is that the causal model is the gameplay. Frostpunk is the anti-source here: six years after release its players still write that "the exact numbers are unknown" and reverse-engineer hope deltas from the observation that ceremonial funerals halve the loss from a death ([zpg §3](research/zpg.md)). Every mood, focus, legitimacy and culture delta is therefore an inspectable named list of contributions, the way RimWorld's thought list and ritual-quality breakdown are ([zpg §4.18](research/zpg.md)).

### The tempo budget

The clock constants in `src/sim.rs` give 24 x 30 x 4 = 2,880 ticks per game year, and the fixed loop runs at 80 ms per tick, so a year is 230 real seconds. Measured against shipped per-citizen sims that is 18x faster than Dwarf Fortress (403,200 ticks per year at the default `[FPS_CAP:100]` is 67 real minutes, and only while the CPU keeps up) and roughly 45x faster than RimWorld at its fastest normal speed (60 days x 16.67 real minutes, x6 cap, so ~2.8 real hours per year). The report puts the target at 30-60x faster than either ([zpg §1b](research/zpg.md)).

Nobody in this genre runs a per-citizen simulation that fast, so per-tick per-citizen work is a hard budget, not a detail. Four rules follow, and every later section is written to respect them.

- Anything O(citizens) that is not needs decay runs on the day or season clock, never the hour clock. Focus recomputes daily, culture drift and trade experience seasonally.
- Culture, votes and window edges read colony-level aggregates maintained incrementally on birth, death and transmission events; nothing scans the population per tick. Stellaris keyed pop groups on ethics in 4.0, the group count exploded, and 4.3 removed the ethics split entirely and replaced it with percentages per group ([culture §4.14](research/culture.md)). That is Paradox conceding on the record that per-carrier ideological drift did not scale.
- Cards stay a bitset per citizen. The per-citizen representation is the fiction and the storage; it is never the query path.
- Nothing is O(cells) per tick and nothing is O(total patches) per tick. Once the world has no edge, both counts grow with the square of how far the colony can walk, so a gathering query is bounded by a search radius over a per-chunk index, regrowth runs over realised chunks only, and any flow field is computed over a bounded tile rather than over the world (section 13).

### Standing constraints

- ADR 0001: headless Bevy, ASCII to the terminal. No GPU, no editor, hand-rolled layout.
- ADR 0002: tick = game hour, 24/30/4, rates declared in game-time units and converted at the edges.
- ADR 0003: threshold-driven *behaviour* uses an enter and a leave threshold. Continuous penalty curves (section 3) are not threshold behaviours and are outside its scope; the band names printed next to them are display only.
- ADR 0008: rustfmt, clippy, test-first, a timed headless run logged in `balance.md` for every change.
- User intents already settled: the mayor is never omnipotent; eugenics-like policies are reachable only after generations of window-shifting; entertainers are load-bearing rather than decorative; tradition cards persist for generations and can be eradicated by harsh methods at a cost that itself imprints new cards.

## 2. Clocks and tempo controller

The calendar is ADR 0002 and does not change. What is new is the accelerator that sits on top of it.

**Interrupt-driven advance, from Aurora 4X** ([zpg §1b](research/zpg.md)). Aurora lets the player pick an increment, tick an Auto Turns box, and run increments of that size until an interrupt event affecting the player fires; interrupts end automated turns and cannot be switched off. Our mapping: the clock advances in the largest increment for which no scheduled event exists, and the horizon is recomputed every sub-step because the simulation creates events inside an increment.

**A pre-roll before the event, from Kerbal Space Program** ([zpg §1b, §2.7](research/zpg.md)). KSP's warp-to-node stops one minute *before* the node rather than at it, and its documented failure is that a stop point computed from a stale forecast overshoots by hours. Our mapping: never advance past the next scheduled event minus one game hour, so the watcher sees the approach rather than the aftermath, and re-check the forecast every sub-step.

**Interrupts are data, not code, from DCSS** ([zpg §1b, §2.6](research/zpg.md)). Crawl ships `explore_stop` as a named list (`items, stairs, shops, altars, portals, branches, runed_doors, ...`) plus per-activity `interrupt_<delay>` lists over named interrupt types, all user-editable. Our mapping: one table keyed by chronicle event variant with a routing of `Pause | Slow | Line | Silent`, editable in a config file. It is the same table section 10 reads, so "worth reading" and "worth slowing for" are one dataset.

RimWorld forces speed back to 1x during any hostile event, which produced years of "disable forced 1x speed" threads and a dedicated mod ([zpg §1b, §3](research/zpg.md)). The lesson taken is that auto-slow is right but its threshold must be tunable, which the routing table already provides.

Open: whether the "few minutes per year" figure is a promise or an average. Option A caps the accelerator so no year ever completes faster than a fixed floor, keeping the pacing legible at the cost of skipping over quiet stretches more slowly. Option B lets tempo be fully event-driven and drops the fixed-year-length claim from the README, which is what an interrupt loop actually produces ([zpg §4.3](research/zpg.md)).

Decision: option B, with a floor. Tempo is event-driven and the README states an average rather than a promise, with one constant setting the minimum real seconds a game year may take so the watcher can still follow it.

## 3. Needs, mood, focus

Three quantities, deliberately not one. Fusing them is the most common failure in the sample: Tropico keeps faction standing, citizen approval and happiness loosely coupled, so players find that "approval has nothing to do with how happy factions are directly" while Tropico 5 players get endless rebellions at 70+ happiness because rebels key off standing instead ([culture §3.10](research/culture.md)).

**Focus is the single currency for every mismatch penalty, from Dwarf Fortress** ([lifecycle §4.1](research/lifecycle.md), [zpg §1d](research/zpg.md)). DF defines focus as "the percentage of the combined totals of met over unmet need values", banded Very focused 140%+ / Focused 101-119% / Unfocused 81-99% / Distracted 61-80% / Badly distracted at or below 60%, worth up to a 50% effective-skill bonus or a 50% penalty. Our mapping is one multiplier with several consequences rather than several parallel debuffs.

```text
effective_stat = base_stat * focus_mult * trade_fit_mult
```

That product drives output, accident chance and construction quality alike. DF also keeps focus explicitly independent of mood — "a dwarf can be happy even with largely-unmet needs, or unhappy yet focused" — and we keep the same split, because focus drives output while mood drives the vote, and fusing them makes every vote a referendum on productivity ([lifecycle §4.1](research/lifecycle.md)).

Conflicts with ADR 0005: the ADR lists four parallel consequences of a mismatch ("costs output, raises fatigue, and increases accidents and discontent"). Collapsing them into one multiplier with four downstream effects is the same fiction with a quarter of the arithmetic, but it is a different mechanism from the one recorded.

Ruling: adopt the single focus multiplier with its four downstream effects; ADR 0005 is amended to match.

**Continuous ramp with a knee, from Timberborn's failure and Workers & Resources' fix** ([lifecycle §4.9](research/lifecycle.md)). Timberborn's binary flips (Starving = -50% work speed, Thirsty = -25% movement) are its single most-cited balance complaint, with a standing request for gradual penalties. W&R ships the better hybrid: unmet demand costs happiness on every roll, with an amplified penalty once the need drops below 5%. Our mapping: each of warmth, rest and hunger contributes a continuous term to focus with a knee near zero, and the printed output is the band name while the value underneath stays continuous.

**Two mood numbers, from RimWorld and DF** ([zpg §4.4](research/zpg.md)). RimWorld computes a mood target instantly from the sum of active thoughts and has the displayed bar chase it asymmetrically at +12 per in-game hour rising and -8 falling, with break thresholds at 35% minor / 20% major / 5% extreme; DF separates short-term stress from a long-term accumulator whose recovery takes years. Our mapping: `mood_target` (instant) and `mood` (chasing, faster up than down), plus a slow `hardship` accumulator with named statuses. At our tempo the chase rates must be declared per game *day*, not per hour, or every citizen sits at target every frame.

**Needs are weighted and have satisfy intervals, from DF** ([zpg §1d](research/zpg.md)). DF weights needs 1/2/5/10 and its rule of thumb is to satisfy a weight-10 need every 3 months, weight-5 every 6 months, weight-2 yearly and weight-1 every two years. Our three needs are the weight-10 tier; the social and recreation needs added in section 9 enter at the lower weights, which is what keeps them from competing with survival in a crisis.

## 4. Lifecycle

**No death by fixed age, from RimWorld and Timberborn** ([lifecycle §4.6](research/lifecycle.md)). RimWorld has no death-by-old-age event at all: elderly pawns accumulate health conditions and lose immunity-gain speed until some illness kills them. Timberborn randomises lifespan by ±10% around a 50-day base. Our mapping: hardiness decays with age on the year clock and each cold snap rolls against current hardiness, which makes winter the agent of death — thematically what this game wants.

This is not decoration. Banished's cohort death wave is the bug we would otherwise inherit for free: births gated on a binary condition (a free house) arrive in bursts, a hard death age makes the matching deaths arrive in bursts 60-70 years later, and players describe an unrecoverable spiral where imported nomads "make only a minimal dent" ([lifecycle §3.1](research/lifecycle.md)). Our current `free_home` gate in `colony_growth` is exactly Banished's step function.

**The age pyramid is an output, from Timberborn** ([lifecycle §4.10](research/lifecycle.md)). High well-being shortens childhood and extends adult life there, so kits fall from 12.00% to 3.12% of the population without the player ever setting a birth rate. Our mapping: warmth and food surplus modulate maturation rate on the year clock, and the terminal prints an age histogram.

Open: how to desynchronise births. Option A smooths the gate into a per-couple seasonal probability scaled by spare housing. Option B keeps the hard free-bed gate exactly as `colony_growth` has it today and relies on randomised lifespan alone to spread the deaths. The report is explicit that at least one of the two is required ([lifecycle §4.10](research/lifecycle.md)).

Decision: both. Births become a per-couple seasonal probability scaled by spare housing, and lifespan is randomised on Timberborn's ±10% shape, so neither the arrivals nor the deaths land in a cohort.

Open: aging rate. Option A ages citizens 1:1 with the calendar and shortens the natural lifespan to fit a run (Timberborn's whole lifespan is 50 days; Norland runs one year per two real days with a max age in the 50s). Option B keeps a realistic lifespan and multiplies aging, as Banished does at 4-5x per calendar year and Workers & Resources at 12 years per in-game year. Banished's multiplier is the genre's most common immersion complaint and spawned a mod called "one year is one year" whose entire purpose is removing it, and the report's conclusion is that a compressed rate must be paired with hiding the calendar ([lifecycle §2.1, §3.8](research/lifecycle.md)). Option B therefore conflicts with ADR 0002, whose whole point is a visible calendar the balance log already prints.

Decision: option A. Aging runs 1:1 with the calendar and the natural lifespan is shortened to fit a run, with frailty from about age 40 and expected death in the 50s, which keeps ADR 0002's visible calendar honest. The compressed-rate option is rejected.

**Inheritance is structural, from Lords & Villeins** ([lifecycle §4.7](research/lifecycle.md)). There the player never commands individuals, "the profession of a family determined what job they can do... cannot be removed", and a spouse marrying in learns the family's profession. Our mapping: ADR 0005's "children inherit a parent's trade" becomes a household property rather than a probability roll, so a new smith requires a smith's child to survive or a deliberate bad fit. The escape valve is mandatory and both Banished and Ostriv ship it: an unassigned laborer pool that absorbs work no household's trade covers, with Ostriv's guidance of holding 20-25% of the workforce unemployed as reserve.

Open: what carries competence. Option A makes the household the unit, as above, so scarcity of a trade is a demographic fact. Option B stores per-citizen experience for every work type, as Ostriv has since 2017, and lets trade be individual. The two produce different stories: A generates dynasties and hard shortages, B generates drift and personal specialisation.

Decision: both, split by role. Competence is stored as per-citizen experience per work type, while the household supplies the inheritance rule: a child takes the household trade by default and starts with a fraction of the head's experience in it. The 20-25% laborer pool is mandatory rather than advisory.

## 5. Stats, jobs, assignment friction, debuffs

**Hidden stats printed as bucketed adjectives, from Dwarf Fortress** ([lifecycle §4.2](research/lifecycle.md)). DF rolls 6 physical and 13 mental attributes on species ranges (dwarven Strength is `[450:950:1150:1250:1350:1550:2250]`), never prints a number, and buckets the description at ±250/500/750/1000 from the *species* median, so "strong" maps to different numbers in different populations. Our mapping: strength, wits and hardiness roll on a fixed range, and the citizen card prints one word per stat, bucketed against the colony median rather than a constant.

Conflicts with ADR 0005: the ADR says per-citizen stats "are only revealed through noisy performance over time", and an adjective on the card *is* the reveal. The resolution the report proposes is to print the adjective only for stats the colony has observed — after N worker-days in a trade that exercises that stat — and to let the printed word be a running estimate that starts wrong and converges. Workers & Resources ships the gated version: loyalty is hidden until the Secret Police install spy equipment in the home.

Ruling: adopt the converging estimate. An adjective is printed only after N observed worker-days and is explicitly the colony's running estimate rather than the true value; ADR 0005 is amended to match.

Where the rolled number itself comes from is settled in section 15, which replaces the roll with a childhood: everything in this section downstream of `base_stat` stands unchanged.

**Job scoring, from Ostriv and Shining Rock** ([lifecycle §4.4](research/lifecycle.md)). Ostriv's `checkForBetterJob` weighs distance, prior experience with that job type, and wage, with a hard 3,500-unit distance cap added after cross-map-commuting complaints; Shining Rock replaced his own closest-job rule with utility scoring for his next game and picks "one of the top ones" rather than the single best. Our mapping: a vacancy is filled by `score = w_dist * distance + w_exp * experience_in_trade + w_lever * mayor_bias`, sampled from the top few rather than argmax, which buys variety with no extra randomness in the model.

Conflicts with ADR 0005: the ADR specifies "the nearest idle citizen takes an open vacancy". Scoring is a different rule. It still produces mismatches — experience is weighted, not required, and the distance term is deliberately the wrong distance — but the recorded decision says nearest, and the manager rules on which stands.

Ruling: adopt job scoring with top-N sampling. "Nearest" survives as the deliberately wrong distance term inside the score rather than as the rule itself; ADR 0005 is amended to match.

**The distance metric is deliberately cheap and wrong, from DF and Banished** ([lifecycle §4.5](research/lifecycle.md)). DF picks the tile with "minimum distance (in the maximum norm) from the current position, regardless of how long the path to actually get there is", and Banished used straight-line distance with the developer publicly naming it as the flaw. Banished 1.0.3 then split the rule by class: laborers have no distance constraint, other professions stay near home and job unless a job is old and no laborer has taken it; W&R caps the daily search at a ~450 m walking radius. Our mapping: haulers feeding construction get an unbounded radius, trade workers a bounded one, with an ageing-job escape valve. This must be narrated in the chronicle or watchers read it as a pathfinding bug, exactly as Banished's did.

**Two fit axes pointing at different buildings, from Against the Storm** ([lifecycle §4.8](research/lifecycle.md)). Proficiency there gives a 10% chance of double output in one tagged building set while Comfort gives +5 Resolve in a *different* tagged set. Our mapping: hidden stats determine output fit, and a separate hidden temperament determines contentment in a trade, feeding mood and therefore the vote. The payoff is that the mayor's best-output assignment is rarely the one citizens would vote for, which is the dramatic engine of the whole game; without it, optimal and popular converge and there is nothing to watch.

**Reassignment costs something, from RimWorld, DF and ONI** ([lifecycle §4.11](research/lifecycle.md)). RimWorld drains skill XP continuously above level 10; DF rusts unused skills and destroys "50% of the gained experience points (rounded down, plus 1)" when retraining a rusty one; ONI inverts it by raising a duplicant's morale need by the skill's tier, reduced by 1 if they are interested in that work. Our mapping: trade experience decays on the season clock above a threshold, so churn is paid for and ADR 0005's "nobody quits a job on their own" stops being arbitrary. This slows recovery from bad assignments, so it ships only alongside section 11.

The one hard interrupt is personal needs. ONI states it flatly — "no matter what priorities you have set, Duplicants will always take care of their personal needs" — every game in the sample has it, and none make it configurable ([lifecycle §2.4](research/lifecycle.md)). `choose_duty` already works this way and does not change.

## 6. Construction and voting

**Construction is gated on material delivery, from Timberborn** ([lifecycle §2.10](research/lifecycle.md)). Timberborn advances a build in 10% steps per material batch delivered and only then spends builder-hours; Workers & Resources prices buildings in workdays instead (a kindergarten is 394-511, a technical university 5,188). Our hauled-timber `Site` already does the delivery-gated version, so layer 5 generalises the existing shape to house, hunter's hut and generator upgrade rather than introducing a new one.

**The vote is on the worst unmet need.** This is our own rule and has no direct source; the mechanic it must avoid is documented, which is Tropico's randomly-generated demands. The militarist faction asking for roller coasters, and two factions arguing "even when they both want the same identical outcome", is the canonical legibility failure ([culture §3.11](research/culture.md)). Every proposal on the ballot therefore has to be derivable from a quantity already visible on the citizens who propose it.

**The mayor is a bias on the tally, not a veto.** ADR 0004 already fixes this: a plain resource of per-building-type weights added to the citizen tally, defaulting to neutral, with no logic of its own. Section 7 is what scales that weight; the voting mechanism itself does not learn about legitimacy.

**Vote thresholds are banded, from Frostpunk 2** ([culture §4.2](research/culture.md)). FP2 seats 100 delegates, needs 51 for an ordinary law and 67 for a *Rule* law that increases the Steward's own power, and treats a Hesitant delegate as a coin flip. Our mapping: proposals that increase the mayor's own reach need the higher bar, which is the cheapest available brake on an omnipotent mayor and needs no new resource.

FP2's other number is a warning rather than a model: a 10-week cooldown between votes, which reviewers describe as turning politics into an errand queue ([culture §1, §3.4](research/culture.md)). Our vote cadence stays on the season clock and every promise made to win a vote resolves inside the same season it was made.

**The ballot is two tiers that are never compared.** First survival: per need, the time a citizen spent already acting on it and still not relieved, summed since the last ballot. A need answered on schedule costs nothing, because a need swinging through its own hysteresis band is control working rather than failing. If any of a citizen's three needs shows a cost there, they vote the worst of those and nothing else enters. Second, efficiency: a citizen the colony is holding up for votes instead on what wastes the most of their day -- the ticks spent walking back to the fire rather than working -- or abstains, which is a legal ballot for a contented citizen. Because the tiers never meet, the two quantities never have to share a unit: a starving colony votes food whatever its haulers are walking, and a comfortable one is free to spend the ballot on efficiency.

A boiler is a productivity building, not a comfort one, which is why it needs the second tier at all. A hauler always makes it back to the warm, so what the cold takes from them is hours, and no measure of how unmet a need got can see an hour.

What the ballot converged on has a name outside games. A need's hysteresis band is a **deadband**: error inside it produces no control action at all, which is exactly why a need swinging through its own band carries no signal. The burden is then the **integral of absolute error taken outside the deadband** -- IAE is the standard integral performance index in controller tuning, alongside ISE, ITAE and ITSE ([Sen et al., error-integral performance indexes](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149197020303504)) -- and "still accumulating while the citizen is already acting" is **integral windup**, the case where the actuator is saturated and the error will not come down ([anti-reset windup](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5270916)). Control engineering treats windup as a pathology to be clamped; here it is the measurement, because a colony wants to know precisely when its own control has saturated. If the excess integral ever needs to weigh recent failures above old ones, the established refinement is **ITAE**, which multiplies the error by elapsed time and so penalises what persists ([Martins, ITAE criterion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0811.0083)); that is the thing to reach for before inventing a weighting.

Four simpler measures were built, measured and rejected, and the record is kept because each rules out a family rather than a value. Reading the shortfall at the instant of the vote hears only from whoever is uncomfortable right then; warmth never won a single vote in a whole run, because citizens walk to the fire before they get cold. Taking the worst shortfall since the last ballot saturates: every need bottoms out at its own low mark by construction, so all three tie at one and the tie-break decides the election -- that version built houses forever and never a hut. Integrating the shortfall ties too, at about half a band per tick, because the integral of a sawtooth does not depend on its period; the same houses-forever result followed. Integrating only the excess past the point of acting does carry the signal -- it elected a boiler within two hundred ticks -- but mixed with a raw detour count it breaks the units, since a tick at the acting threshold then contributes nothing while a detour tick still contributes one, and the colony built boilers it could not fuel. The two-tier split keeps the excess measure and dissolves the unit question by never asking the two to compare.

## 7. Mayor: budget, legitimacy, Overton window, contest

**Influence is a scarce, unreliable budget, from Godville** ([zpg §1a, §4.12](research/zpg.md)). Godville's godpower is a 0-200% bar priced at Voice of God 5%, Encourage 25%, Punish 25%, Miracle 50%, so a full bar buys two to four interventions; almost every lever is probabilistic (voice commands land about 30% of the time in the Arena, Encourage can backfire and heal the monster) and the single deterministic lever, Miracle, is gated behind a temple costing 1,000 gold bricks at one brick per 3,000 coins spent. Godville also enforces windows with no influence at all. Our mapping: legitimacy is the budget, it refills slowly, each nudge spends it, and exactly one deterministic action exists and is expensive. At least one recurring window — a crisis, a festival — locks the mayor out entirely.

Conflicts with ADR 0004: the ADR makes the mayor a set of weights *added* to the vote tally, so influence scales the magnitude of a bias. Godville's model makes legitimacy set the *probability* that a directive is followed. These are different mechanisms and only one can be the primary channel. Whichever wins, an ignored directive must emit a chronicle line naming why it was ignored, or unreliability reads as a bug ([zpg §4.12](research/zpg.md)).

Ruling: both, on different channels. Votes keep additive weights whose magnitude legitimacy scales, while directives and policies are obeyed probabilistically with legitimacy as the probability. ADR 0004 stands and is amended to say so, the probabilistic channel is recorded in the new mayor ADR, and an ignored directive always emits a chronicle line naming why.

Open: what the mayor's one economic lever actually is. Ostriv gives the player a town-wide basic wage, a managers' wage, a laborers' wage and a per-building wage percentage, and citizens choose for themselves; the developer's stated reason for refusing direct assignment is that "you can't just assign someone to work in specific place and live with specific spouse because, you know, that's mathematically effective" ([lifecycle §4.3](research/lifecycle.md)). We have no money, so option A is a firewood ration per workplace and option B is hearth priority, meaning how close to the generator that workplace's crew may sleep. Ostriv also ships the failure mode: set the laborers' wage too low and construction starves because everyone prefers a vacant regular slot.

Decision: option A. The lever is a firewood ration per workplace, a visible number that can be voted on and that ties straight into the fire economy. Hearth priority is a later spatial refinement, not the first lever.

**Legitimacy is throughput, not a permission bit, from Old World and Victoria 3** ([culture §2.5, §1](research/culture.md)). Old World converts legitimacy directly into Orders per turn. Victoria 3 bands it Illegitimate below 25 / Unacceptable 25-49 / Contested 50-74 / Legitimate 75-89 / Righteous 90+, with Illegitimate generating +3.0% radical pops against Righteous at +0.05% and -25% opposition approval, and it is the one shipped mechanic in the sample that *forbids* rather than prices: below 25 a government cannot pass laws at all unless a movement endorsing that law has at least 25 activism. Our mapping: legitimacy multiplies the mayor's weight and sets the width of the fringe band described below.

That concentration is exactly what CK3's legitimacy was punished for, where activity spam is the only reliable source, losses are asymmetric, and the most popular workshop mod removes the feature outright ([culture §1, §3.7](research/culture.md)). So legitimacy needs at least three independent, non-spammable inflows: surviving a named crisis, fulfilling a granted petition, and delivering a promise made to win a vote ([culture §4.7](research/culture.md)).

**Position gates the policy set, from Tropico 7** ([culture §4.17](research/culture.md)). After seven years of a faction layer that never worked, Kalypso's fix is that "available edicts depend on where El Presidente is located in the Political Matrix" — "you cannot enact a very left-wing law if you have previously chosen a very right-wing constitution" — with the only override being a faction deal earned by fulfilling that faction's demands. Our mapping: adopt it wholesale, including the rule that the override is earned by service to the objectors and never bought with a generic currency. This supersedes any temptation to let legitimacy alone buy an out-of-window policy.

**Never depose instantly, from Frostpunk and Norland** ([culture §4.8](research/culture.md)). Frostpunk 1 gives an ultimatum to move the offending meter 15% within 2 days; Frostpunk 2 demands Trust climb from 0% to 25%; Norland offers to sacrifice a lord before the fanatics march at average peasant mood below 25. Our mapping: legitimacy at zero opens a Grace season with a concrete demand and a named scapegoat option — dismiss an official, repeal a named policy, hand over a card's leading carrier. In a zero-player game this is the moment the watcher most wants to intervene, so the resolution must be reachable by nudges rather than by commands, or the premise breaks at its most interesting moment ([zpg §4.11](research/zpg.md)).

**Contestation is a named pipeline, from Democracy 4 and Frostpunk 2** ([culture §4.10](research/culture.md)). D4 escalates angry voter to pressure group to radicalised member to terrorist organisation; FP2 splinters a neglected community into a more extreme faction. Our stages are petition, work-to-rule, quiet sabotage, council override, exile, and each stage exposes one de-escalation action that costs a *different* resource than the one under pressure: grant the petition costs build capacity, buy off costs food, suppress costs legitimacy.

**Unrest scale and unrest participants are separate queries, from Tropico 5** ([culture §4.20](research/culture.md)). Its datamined model sets a colony-level Rebellion Threat that fixes scale and frequency (L2: 14 rebels, 15% chance; L3: 20 and 30%; L4: 26 and 45%; L5: 33 and 60%), recounted every 2 months with an attack rolled only if 12 months have passed since the last, while *who* rebels is a per-citizen query on lowest happiness with Liberty counted twice and "Approval has nothing to do with this elevation". Our mapping: colony Unrest sets how large and how often contestation fires, with a minimum interval so protest cannot spam; participants are drawn separately from the carriers of the offended card.

**Petitions name a concrete cost, from Dwarf Fortress** ([culture §4.11](research/culture.md)). DF prices a temple or guildhall petition as a room worth 2,000☼, or 6,000☼ on a hard economy and 10,000☼ for a grand guildhall; accepting gives a positive thought and denying a negative one that "can make it into memory" and persists. Our mapping: every petition names a buildable or enactable thing with a visible cost, and denial imprints a card on the petitioners. This is the cheapest source of card births available and requires no event authoring.

## 8. Culture axes and tradition cards

ADR 0006 sets three axes: collectivist-individualist, pious-secular, harsh-lenient. Frostpunk 2 independently landed on three (Adaptation-Progress, Equality-Merit, Tradition-Reason), so the count is not the open question ([culture §1](research/culture.md)).

**One position per citizen, from Tropico 7's correction of Tropico 6** ([culture §4.18](research/culture.md)). Tropico 6 let a citizen hold a slot on each of four axes and averaged their approval, which produced ultimatums from factions with zero members and standing decoupled from size; Tropico 7 reverts to one faction per citizen "making their demands and position easier to understand". Our mapping: a citizen has exactly one vector on the three axes. Cards shift that vector and supply the vocabulary for objections, but a citizen is never a member of several blocs whose demands get averaged.

**The window is computed, not a constant, from Democracy 4** ([culture §4.1](research/culture.md)). D4 blends each voter's innate position with the national consensus as `Final = Global * Impressionability + Innate * (1 - Impressionability)`, with Impressionability drawn from a normal distribution clamped to 15%-85% so the population can never become uniform. Our mapping: per axis, derive the window edges from percentiles of the live citizen distribution. The clamp is what guarantees contestation survives generations of drift.

**Three bands, not a binary gate, from Frostpunk 2** ([culture §4.2](research/culture.md)). Inside the window is an ordinary vote; a fringe band at the outer edge passes only with a supermajority or a promise, and always draws contestation; outside is refused.

Conflicts with ADR 0006: the ADR says a policy outside the window is "refused with protest and a loss of legitimacy", flat. Nobody in the sample ships a hard window — D4 lets anything pass with enough political capital, SMAC prices simultaneity on an 8n³ curve at 32/108/256/500 for one to four changes, CK3 charges +50% cost and +25% time to *replace* a tradition rather than fill an empty slot, Frostpunk 1 charges irreversibility ([culture §2.1](research/culture.md)). Keeping a hard outer edge is defensible, but without a fringe band in front of it the mayor's only legible move at the boundary is to do nothing.

Ruling: adopt three bands, with the fringe band in front of a hard outer edge; ADR 0006 is amended to match.

**Two clocks on every policy, from Democracy 4** ([culture §4.3](research/culture.md)). D4 splits immediate anger from slow normalisation: legalising cannabis angers conservatives on the turn it passes, but its long-run effect is "increased liberalism, and people get used to the idea". Our mapping: each policy carries a `shock` (an immediate mood and legitimacy delta scaled by each citizen's axis distance from the policy) and a `normalisation` (a small per-season pull of the window edge toward the policy, applied only while the policy remains in force).

**Cynicism closes the enact-repeal exploit, from Democracy 4** ([culture §4.4](research/culture.md)). D4's Cynicism rises from flip-flopping, from bribing voters before an election and from hurting them after one, and decays only by being forgotten. Our mapping: repealing a policy within N seasons of enacting it raises colony Cynicism, which caps legitimacy *gain* until it ages out. Without this, the two-clock model above becomes a free window-shifting engine.

**Anti-ratchet decay toward a card-derived baseline, from CK3** ([culture §4.5](research/culture.md)). CK3 acceptance decays -0.01/year for every point above a statically computed baseline, so drift pulls toward a structural equilibrium instead of clamping. Our mapping: the tradition-card carrier distribution computes a baseline window and the live window decays toward it each season. Policies move the live window; only cards move the baseline. That is precisely how "eugenics is generations away" becomes a state condition rather than an invisible countdown — card composition is the generational clock, the live window the tactical one.

**The harsh policy is gated on card composition, not a timer** ([culture §4.15](research/culture.md)). SMAC has the sample's only genuine hard prohibition — a faction may not select its aversion at all — but that is a fixed identity trait, not a moving boundary. Our mapping: such a policy becomes proposable only when a prerequisite card exceeds a carrier fraction *and* the harsh edge of the window has crossed a threshold, both of which the watcher can read off the screen.

**Cards are few and each must change a decision, from RimWorld** ([zpg §4.6](research/zpg.md), [culture §1](research/culture.md)). A RimWorld ideoligion holds at most 4 memes, a fluid one starts with a single non-high-impact meme, and reform costs escalate at 10 points then 12. CK3 1.13 rebalanced 40+ traditions and "removed some inconsequential modifiers", which is Paradox conceding a chunk of its own list was filler ([culture §3.6](research/culture.md)). Our mapping: cap the active card set at 4-6 so the colony's culture reads as a sentence.

Open: where cards come from. Option A buys them with ritual outcomes, as RimWorld's fluid ideoligion earns development points from rituals and conversions, which makes section 9's entertainers the source of cultural change. Option B lets cards be born only from colony events and denied petitions, as DF's denial "can make it into memory", which keeps the culture reactive and needs no currency. The two produce very different entertainers.

Decision: option B for births. Cards are born from colony events and denied petitions with no development-point currency; entertainers instead multiply transmission and normalisation, and rituals keep paying mood, a work-speed buff and axis pushes without ever buying a card.

**Eradication is priced in a second currency and imprints, from Tropico 3, Frostpunk 2 and Stellaris** ([culture §4.12, §4.21](research/culture.md)). Tropico 3's Outlaw Faction costs $15,000 and "up to half" of the removed supporters become rebels, while Book BBQ removes 50% of Intellectual supporters *and* halves the immigration chance for that leaning — stock and inflow both. FP2's Round Up arrests up to 500 faction members and causes "a large increase in Tension"; Stellaris Suppress cuts attraction 75% but costs 50% approval. Our mapping: eradicating a card strips it from carriers, cuts its transmission rate at birth and by contact, converts a fraction of ex-carriers into active contestants, and stamps a Fear of Authority card on every witness. That new card widens the harsh edge of the window while reducing all legitimacy gain.

That asymmetry is the direct fix for Frostpunk 1's degenerate loop, where control laws can be escalated until "hope will never be a problem again" and the only penalty is a judgemental epilogue ([culture §3.1](research/culture.md)). Harshness must buy room and cost standing, or the loop collapses. The second half of the fix is RimWorld's leaky bucket: slave suppression decays by up to 20% per day against a 45-day base rebellion interval, so coercion is an ongoing upkeep rather than a one-time purchase ([culture §1](research/culture.md)). And Tropico 5's "killing a non-rebel by order: +1 threat" is the cleanest precedent for suppression feeding the number it is meant to reduce ([culture §4.20](research/culture.md)).

**Dying cards re-concentrate, then the bonus caps, from CK3** ([culture §4.13](research/culture.md)). Below 40% fervor an organised CK3 faith risks a heresy converting 10-25 counties, and every ruler who defects returns +15% fervor to the religion they left — losing the extremes re-concentrates the core.

Conflicts with ADR 0006: the ADR says traditions die "with their last carrier". A re-concentration bonus makes the tail harder to kill. Resolution: cap the bonus so extinction stays reachable but never cheap, and apply the eradication transmission penalty as a multiplier *after* the small-population bonus, so persecution still concentrates the faithful but cannot outrun a sustained campaign.

Ruling: adopt the capped re-concentration bonus with the eradication transmission penalty applied after it, which leaves "dies with its last carrier" literally true; ADR 0006 is amended to match.

## 9. Entertainers

The anti-source first, because it is the exact failure this layer must avoid. Dwarf Fortress ships a full Performer occupation with musician, singer, poet, dancer and speaker skills, generated musical and poetic forms, and visiting troupes, and its own wiki concludes that "performers serve little practical purpose, as they mostly just duplicate the behavior of other taverngoers" — assigned performers do not perform measurably more than unassigned dwarves ([zpg §1d, §3](research/zpg.md)). Generative depth creates nothing; only changing an outcome does.

**Quality is a sum of named contributions with an outcome distribution, from RimWorld rituals** ([zpg §4.5](research/zpg.md)). A RimWorld dance party assembles Quality from listed terms — 10 participants +30%, started at a lightball +20%, six loudspeakers +30%, room impressiveness 120 +20% — mapping to outcomes at Terrible = 1/(4+16Q), Boring = 3/(4+16Q), Fun = 3Q/(1+4Q), Unforgettable = Q/(1+4Q), paying -3 / -1 / +8 / +16 mood, with Fun or better carrying a 90% chance of +20% global work speed for 24 hours. Roles matter mechanically there: a moral guide present is +30% quality on a funeral. Our mapping: a performance's Quality comes from audience size, venue warmth, entertainer skill and tradition match; the outcome tier pays in mood, in a colony-wide work-speed buff for a fixed number of days, and in culture points.

The tuning conflict is stated up front because it is where the numbers will be hardest: a labour-hour spent entertaining is a labour-hour not spent on wood, so the payout has to be marginal in a crisis and dominant on a plateau ([zpg §4.5](research/zpg.md)).

**Cap what entertainment advertises, from The Sims' own postmortem** ([zpg §1a, §3](research/zpg.md)). The Sims' object-advertisement model has agents pick the behaviour maximising current happiness with a distance attenuation of 0.3 and an autonomy threshold of 60 per interaction; the team's documented failure is that "early versions of the Joy Booth were so addictive that Sims would continue using it until they collapsed". Zubek names the two companion failures: distance attenuation producing bird-in-hand behaviour, and false advertisements creating unbreakable action loops. Our mapping: the score entertainment advertises is bounded and falls as the need fills, and the arbiter is weighted-random over the top N, which Zubek calls "a good compromise between having some predictability about what will happen, and not having the agent look unpleasantly deterministic". That is the same top-N sampling as section 5's job scoring, so it is one helper.

**Monotony is modelled and expectations rise with prosperity, from RimWorld recreation** ([zpg §4.7](research/zpg.md)). Recreation there pays +10 mood at 85-100%, +5 at 70-85%, nothing at 30-70%, -5 at 15-30%, -10 at 0-15% and -20 at zero; the need falls 2.5%/hour, tolerance rises by two-thirds of the gain, a pawn is bored of a type above 50% tolerance and un-bored below 30%, and tolerance decay runs 18%/day below 15k wealth down to 7%/day above 308k, with the game recommending 2 recreation types at low wealth rising to 6 at high. Our mapping: the number of distinct entertainment kinds needed to hold mood scales with prosperity per capita, not with absolute prosperity, because a small colony cannot field the variety a large one can.

Tropico adds the last term: entertainment buildings are scored on service *quality*, not on mere availability, so a bad venue does not satisfy the need ([zpg §1d](research/zpg.md)).

## 10. Chronicle and legibility

**Chronicle entries come from explicit hooks, from CK2** ([zpg §4.1](research/zpg.md)). CK2's Chronicle is not a filter over the message feed; it is a separately authored stream written by `on_action` hooks on a small set of notable event types — births and deaths, wars, marriages, title changes, conversions, imprisonments. The contrast is DF's announcement system, which is fully configurable per type across Popup / Pause / Recenter / Alert switches and whose own wiki warns "don't get ambitious and change all the combat events to Alert, you'll regret it!" ([zpg §1c](research/zpg.md)). Our mapping: a closed `ChronicleEvent` enum with a hand-written template per variant, and every other simulation event stays out. The chronicle is deliberately lossy and a separate debug log exists for development.

**A rolling feed and a sparse permanent record are different objects, from Godville and CK2** ([zpg §4.14](research/zpg.md)). Godville's diary holds exactly 10 entries, 20 with the Book of Creation, and discards older lines, so the observation window is a 10-15 minute rolling feed rather than an archive.

Conflicts with ADR 0007: the ADR makes "an event log under the map" the primary interface. At 230 real seconds per game year a feed scrolls faster than anyone reads, so comprehension has to be carried by a sparse permanent record browsable by year, with the feed reduced to a peripheral ticker. The ADR's conclusion that naming individual citizens becomes necessary survives either way.

Ruling: the sparse permanent record is the primary carrier of comprehension and the rolling feed is a peripheral ticker; ADR 0007 is amended to match.

**A three-scale event ribbon, from RimWorld's History screen** ([zpg §4.9](research/zpg.md)). RimWorld runs a ribbon of coloured circles above its wealth and population graphs, one per event, blue or white for positive-or-neutral, yellow for negative, red for attacks, filterable to the last 300 / 100 / 30 days. Our mapping: one line of glyphs per season under the map, coloured by valence, expandable to per-day and per-hour. Prose cannot answer "what kind of year was this" at our tempo; this can.

Open: whether the ribbon uses colour. Option A uses stable colour semantics and requires a colour-capable terminal. Option B encodes valence in glyph shape only, keeping the output pure text at the cost of a slower read.

Decision: both, glyph first. Valence is encoded in glyph shape as the primary channel and colour reinforces it where the terminal supports it, so the monochrome output stays readable on its own.

**Budget the terminal and make the sidebar the index, from Brogue** ([zpg §4.16](research/zpg.md)). Brogue fixes 100 columns by 34 rows: a 20-column sidebar, a 79x29 map, 3 message lines and two reserved bottom rows, one permanently flavour text. The sidebar lists entities nearest-first at two rows each, so about 16 fit, and each screen row maps back to a map position so the sidebar doubles as the cycle target list. Our mapping: a fixed sidebar of the citizens who currently matter most — which makes ranking mandatory, since a 40-citizen colony cannot fit — plus a bounded chronicle area with an archive key. Brogue's message handling supplies the rest: a `--MORE--` gate, a 340-line archive, and repeats collapsed into a counter capped at 100.

**Two RNG streams and contrast enforced in code, from Brogue** ([zpg §4.17](research/zpg.md)). Brogue splits `RNG_COSMETIC` from `RNG_SUBSTANTIVE` so shimmering terrain never perturbs seed reproducibility, and `separateColors()` rejects foreground/background pairs below `MIN_COLOR_DIFF 600` on a Rec.709-weighted metric, iterating up to 10 rounds until the glyph separates. Our mapping: both, directly. Cheap, and it prevents an entire class of irreproducible-run bugs.

**The chronicle feeds back into the colony, from RimWorld and Caves of Qud** ([zpg §4.10](research/zpg.md)). RimWorld generates sculpture descriptions from a grammar mixing pre-written fragments with actual past colony events — killings, social fights, a person recruited, an animal trained, escaping a roof collapse — so the log is engraved on objects that then sit in rooms and generate mood. Our mapping: entertainers turn past chronicle entries into songs, so a bad winter becomes a cultural asset a decade later. The constraint is template fatigue: a small grammar over a long run is recognisable, so grammar size scales with expected session length.

## 11. Anti-spiral and ending

This section ships before the mechanics that make spirals possible, not after. DF's tantrum spiral is the genre's cautionary tale and the wiki describes the outcome precisely: "the player may be left unable to take any action to salvage the situation, and such a fort's downfall is often from the player abandoning after getting bored staring helplessly at a screen for months" ([lifecycle §3.2](research/lifecycle.md)). In a zero-player game the risk is strictly worse than in DF, because the watcher cannot intervene at all — an unrecoverable spiral is not a hard-won loss, it is several minutes of watching a spreadsheet die.

**Difficulty is budgeted from the colony's own success, from RimWorld** ([zpg §1 storyteller, §2.2](research/zpg.md)). RimWorld computes `Raid Points = (Wealth Points + Pawn Points) x Difficulty x Starting Factor x Adaptation Factor`, floor 35, cap 10,000, with a Starting Factor of 0.7 for the first 10 days reaching 1.0 at day 40 and an Adaptation Factor of 0.4-1.47 that rises with time since the last colonist death and drops when one occurs. Sylvester's stated rule is that "the game tries to hurt but not kill the colony". Our mapping: the severity of winters and crises is a budget computed from population and stockpile, recent deaths buy grace, and the first year is scaled down.

**Expectations fall after losses, from RimWorld** ([lifecycle §4.12](research/lifecycle.md)). Its three named mitigations are threats scaling to wealth, expectations adjusting downward after losses so survivors need less to stay stable, and a one-time scripted rescue for hopeless states. Our mapping: after a mass-casualty winter, drop the baseline need thresholds for survivors for N seasons. This blunts the tragedy the theme is built on, and that trade is accepted deliberately.

**Plan the ending, from Universal Paperclips** ([zpg §4.15, §1a](research/zpg.md)). Lantz on finiteness: "I really wanted to kind of put the hooks in and then let people off the hook", and "one of the things I'm most proud of is that the ending's good". The counter-cases are Clicker Heroes 2, whose own postmortem says "we spent much more money on the project than we made from it" before it was delisted, and WorldBox, where sessions with no goal collapse into using the destructive tools because destruction is the only instant feedback. Sylvester supplies the framing: "Loss is an essential part of a story, not its conclusion". Our mapping: colony death is a chronicle chapter with a legible epitaph, not a game-over screen.

Open: what the terminal state is. Option A keeps the README's premise — the forest is finite, so every run ends frozen — and invests entirely in making the ending readable. Option B adds a reachable survival end, the winter that ends or the city that outlives its founders, so a run has two distinguishable outcomes and the anti-spiral work has something to protect.

Decision: option B. A run has two outcomes: colony death stays a chronicle chapter with an epitaph, and a reachable survival end arrives with the seasons layer, either surviving a fixed number of winters or the city outliving its founders.

One more failure worth naming: full autonomy with no override reads as unfairness rather than difficulty. Timberborn players report beavers dying of thirst next to water, including pump operators who will not drink at their own pump, and its two top-voted requests are for manual assignment ([lifecycle §3.3](research/lifecycle.md)). The closer the player is to zero control, the more the simulation must visibly explain its own bad decisions — which is why section 5's deliberately wrong distance metric is only affordable alongside section 10's chronicle.

## 13. A world with no edge

The three sections that follow were written against a new report, `research/worldgen.md`, and they answer the two intents that opened them: the world has no edge, and a citizen's stats are raised rather than rolled.

**The disc is a renderer, not a boundary.** The simulation reaches for `R` in exactly four places — its own definition, `CENTER`, `PATCH_RADIUS` and `PLOT_MAX_RADIUS` — and every other use of it in the crate is in `window.rs` or in a test. The count has held through the chunked world, though not without a scare: a fifth site appeared as the distance fallback in `assign_trades`, which reached for `R` because there was no other number to mean "far", and it went again the moment there was one. Nothing clamps a position and no system asks whether a cell is on the map — `step_toward` is `from + (to - from).signum()` and will walk to any coordinate an `IVec2` can hold. Content placement is no longer bounded either: a chunk is drawn where somebody looks and dropped when nobody is near, under a ceiling of `WORLD_CELLS_HELD` cells that the headless build asserts every tick. The bound that is left is the window's: it spawns a fixed board of `(R * 2 + 1)^2` = 1,369 sprite and text pairs at startup, masks it with `inside`, repaints every one of them every tick, and clips anything standing outside it. What remains is therefore a camera problem alone.

**Heat survives for free, and that is what makes the rest affordable.** `Air::heat_at` is `(output - dist(p, CENTER) * HEAT_FALLOFF).max(0.0) + ambient`: one distance, no field, no storage, evaluable at any coordinate in any order. A warm ring that is a closed-form function of position is exactly the thing an unbounded world can keep. What breaks the property is the second heat source, which is why section 14's waystations are a tempo question and not only an economy one.

**A chunk must be a pure function of the seed and its own coordinates, from Minecraft's own bug class** ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)). Chunk decoration there historically wrote features across chunk borders into neighbours that did not exist yet, forcing those to generate and sometimes cascading further — the community named it cascading worldgen lag, and modern generation staggers each chunk through discrete recorded statuses (`empty` → `structure_starts` → ... → `features` → `full`) precisely to bound how much of a neighbour has to exist before it can be touched. Our mapping: `noise(seed, salt)` is already a stateless splitmix64 hash of two integers, so `noise(hash(chunk_x, chunk_y), salt)` can be evaluated in any order and thrown away freely. What has to be forbidden, in writing, before the first piece of content wants it, is any placement rule that consults an adjacent chunk. `Lineage` is the counter-example already in the tree: it hands out sequential seeds and is order-dependent by construction, so nothing generated lazily may draw from it.

**Store the delta, regenerate the rest, and note that Minecraft does not** ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)). Minecraft persists generated chunks to 32x32-chunk region files as NBT rather than recomputing them, because block edits, entities and tile data would otherwise be lost; seed-plus-coordinate determinism is real there and is what pre-generation tools rely on, but it is not the live game's persistence path. Our mutable world state is small and enumerable — a patch's `amount` against its generated `cap`, and whatever has been built — so we can take the cheaper half: a sparse map from coordinate to delta, with everything else coming back from the seed. We can afford this because we have no block edits, and saying why is what keeps someone from adding one.

**Our noise is a hash, not a field, and the difference matters** ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)). Gradient noise assigns a random gradient at each lattice point and interpolates dot products; value noise assigns a random value and interpolates that; either evaluates at any coordinate with no prior state, at 2^n corner evaluations in n dimensions. `noise(seed, salt)` is a value *hash* with no continuity at all between adjacent cells, which is right for a per-patch roll and wrong for a terrain field, where two neighbouring cells must resemble each other. The adaptation is to interpolate between lattice hashes rather than to sample per cell, and the honest description of the result is value noise on a coarse lattice, not Perlin.

**Whittaker's diagram has two axes and we have one, so state the substitution** ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)). Whittaker places terrestrial biomes in the plane of mean annual temperature against mean annual precipitation, each biome occupying a bounded region of that plane. The games use more: Minecraft 1.18 looks a column up in continentalness, erosion, temperature, humidity, weirdness and depth; Dwarf Fortress seeds elevation, rainfall, temperature, drainage, volcanism and wildness on a variable-size grid and derives vegetation from elevation, rainfall and temperature together. Our temperature axis is not a field: `ambient_at` is global to the whole world and `heat_at` is a closed form of distance from one point, so the entire colony stands in one climate and Whittaker's temperature axis collapses to a constant. What survives is the *shape* — a biome as a bounded region of a parameter plane — with one generated axis, which the diagram's second axis names for us as how much a cell grows, and distance from the hearth as the other. That is a two-axis lookup where the second axis is not generated at all, and it is the whole of the adaptation.

**Distance from the hearth is the risk axis and the reward axis at once.** Patch richness rises with distance while the warmth cost of reaching it rises linearly, so the expected yield of a trip has an interior maximum that moves with the season and with what the fire is putting out. Without that gradient an unbounded world is decoration, because a colony with a regrowing near ring has no reason ever to leave it — which is the failure section 14 inherits from RimWorld's caravans.

Conflicts with section 1: the tempo budget is written entirely in citizens, because the cell count was a constant nobody had to think about. It is no longer. `gather_source` linear-scans `Patches` for every gathering citizen every tick, which is O(citizens x patches) — 30 x 12 = 360 comparisons today. Patch count grows with realised area and area grows with the square of reach, so at today's density of twelve patches over the roughly 908 cells inside the treeline ring, a fifty-cell world holds about a hundred patches and a two-hundred-cell world about sixteen hundred, which is fifty thousand comparisons a tick at thirty citizens. Section 1 needs a fourth rule naming the spatial axis, and the queries need bounding: a search radius and a per-chunk index instead of a scan, `regrow_patches` over realised chunks only. The precedent is standard — `HPA*` is about 10x faster than an optimised `A*` for paths within 1% of optimal by searching an abstract graph over clusters, and flow field tiles compute one field over a *bounded* region and share it across agents ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)). A flow field over an unbounded world is not a technique.

Ruling: the budget gains a fourth rule, on the spatial axis it never had. Nothing is O(cells) per tick and nothing is O(total patches) per tick; a gathering query is bounded by a search radius over a per-chunk index; regrowth runs over realised chunks only; and any flow field is computed over a bounded tile rather than over the world. Section 1 is amended to carry the fourth rule alongside the three it already has.

Conflicts with ADR 0014: the ADR fixes a board of `(R * 2 + 1)` cells spawned once at startup and repainted whole every tick, and an unbounded world has no such number. The replacement is a viewport that follows the colony, which makes the renderer's per-tick cost O(viewport) rather than O(map) — cheaper than what ships today, not more expensive. What the ADR loses is the property it was built for: the disc drawn round on square cells, centred, entire. A camera means the watcher can no longer see the whole colony at once, and the heat map, which is the one thing the window renderer exists to show, is exactly the thing that reaches past the edge of any viewport.

Ruling: a viewport rather than a board, repainted at O(viewport); ADR 0014 is amended. What the viewport is anchored to is the last fork of this section, and that decision is what carries the loss of the whole-disc view.

Conflicts with ADR 0005: the ADR calls the distance metric "deliberately cheap and wrong", but with king-moves via `signum` the Chebyshev norm that `gather_source` ranks on is the exactly correct travel time, and the wrongness is latent rather than live. Two things make it real. First, `heat_at` measures Euclidean distance while movement measures Chebyshev, so the warm ring is round and the walk is square, and the gap between the two grows without bound with distance — on a diagonal the Euclidean distance is about 1.41 times the Chebyshev one, which is seven cells of disagreement at the current rim and twenty at fifty cells out. Second, the moment terrain has a cost the straight-line estimate diverges from the path cost, which is Banished's documented flaw and is unbounded in the distance. ADR 0005 also gives haulers an unbounded search radius, which was affordable when the map was eighteen cells across and is the query that has to be bounded first.

Ruling: split by what the number is for. Movement and every decision a citizen makes from it — the return lookahead, job and patch scoring, the marginal-value arithmetic of section 14 — use the Chebyshev metric, which is travel-true under king-moves and therefore not wrong at all. The heat field stays Euclidean, because that is physics rather than a decision. The divergence between the round ring and the square walk is accepted, and the chronicle must narrate it on the occasions it bites, exactly as ADR 0005 already requires of the cheap metric. The hauler's unbounded radius is bounded twice over: by the realised-chunk index, and by the marginal-value cutoff that decides when a patch is too far to be worth the walk. ADR 0005 is amended to say which metric serves which purpose.

**Precision is a real bound and it is far away, which is the reason to write it down now** ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)). Single-precision accuracy halves for every power of two from the origin and cannot represent fractions past 8,388,608 units; Minecraft's Far Lands began at about 12,550,821 because a noise value accumulating roughly 171.103 per block overflowed, and were fixed in Beta 1.8 by a modulo 2^24 wraparound on the noise coordinates; Kerbal Space Program's Deep Space Kraken is the same failure in physics, fixed by making the active vessel the coordinate origin and moving the universe around it. Our positions are `IVec2` and stay integer; the only f32 in the spatial path is `heat_at`'s `as_vec2().distance()`, which is exact while coordinates stay under 2^24. Section 14's leash puts the colony's reach at fifty-odd cells, so the bound is six orders of magnitude away and no floating origin is needed. It is worth recording only because a later mechanic that moves the hearth, or a second colony, would start eating that headroom silently.

Open: how big a chunk is. Option A takes a small chunk, 16 cells square like Minecraft's, so the realised set tracks the colony closely and a hauler forty cells out has pulled in about nine chunks. Option B takes a chunk large enough that the whole reachable world is a handful of them — 64 cells square, so the fifty-cell leash lives inside a 3x3 — which makes the realised set nearly static and the per-chunk index nearly pointless, at the cost of generating far more content than anyone visits. The two differ in whether chunk management is a live system or a one-time cost.

Decision: option B, 64 cells square. Chunk management becomes a one-time cost rather than a live system, which is the cheaper thing to be right about; the per-chunk index survives regardless, because its job is bounding queries rather than bounding memory.

Open: whether the world is genuinely unbounded or very large. Option A is truly unlimited, which is what the user intent asks for literally and which means no code anywhere may assume a maximum coordinate. Option B is a bound placed so far out that nothing reaches it, which is what Minecraft actually ships — its border sits at 29,999,984, one chunk short of the engine's 30,000,000, specifically so players cannot fall through the world at the true edge ([worldgen §1a](research/worldgen.md)) — and which lets the balance log assert that a run stayed inside a known box. Every finite-world game in the sample is option B in some form: Terraria at 8400x2400 tiles on a large world, Caves of Qud at 80x25 parasangs of 3x3 zones, Songs of Syx at 768x768.

Decision: option B, with the bound placed absurdly far out — on the order of 2^20 cells — asserted by the headless instrument and relied on by nothing else. Unbounded in play, bounded in proof. It also keeps the f32 heat path four orders of magnitude inside its 2^24 headroom without any code having to think about it.

Open: what happens to a patch when its chunk stops being realised. Option A keeps every patch the colony has ever seen in the live `Patches` vector, so a treeline cut out in year two is still cut out in year nine and the world remembers everything, at a storage cost linear in explored area. Option B keeps only the delta against what the seed generates and lets an untouched chunk fall back to its generated state, which is Minecraft's determinism property without Minecraft's persistence, and which means regrowth in an unrealised chunk is free but a partially cut treeline needs a stored number. The two differ in whether the colony can exhaust a world or only the part of it that it is standing in.

Decision: a hybrid, and it is stated as one rather than as either option. A patch the colony has *touched* keeps its delta permanently, so the store is sparse in the only sense that matters — it holds what was worked, not what was seen. An untouched cell always regenerates from the seed and costs nothing. Option B taken literally would let a colony cut a treeline out, walk away until the chunk stopped being realised, and come back to a full one, which is an abandon-and-return exploit rather than a memory policy.

Open: whether the watcher follows the colony or the map. Option A gives the window a camera locked to the hearth, so the picture is the same one that ships today and citizens simply walk off the edge of it, with the status lines reporting how many are out. Option B follows the population's centre of mass or the furthest expedition, so the watcher sees the walk but loses the fixed frame that makes the heat map legible as a ring. ADR 0014's whole argument was that square cells make the disc round; neither option keeps a disc.

Decision: option A, locked to the hearth. Citizens walk off the frame and the status lines report how many are out, which is a legible thing to read and a cheap thing to draw. A view that follows an expedition is a later iteration of the window and belongs in the v2 queue, not here.

## 14. Expeditions, the leash, and dying out of sight

Once there is no rim, a hauler can walk until the cold takes them, and the design question is what stops them. It must not be a scripted return order: an order is a rule the watcher has to be told about, and the colony already carries a quantity that answers the question by itself.

**The leash is already in the constants.** Warmth falls 1 an hour outside the freezing line and climbs 2 an hour inside it, from a ceiling of 100 to a death at 0. The fire reaches `(GENERATOR_HEAT - |ambient|) / HEAT_FALLOFF` cells, which is 13 on an average day and 9 at the winter floor, and every cell past that costs one warmth going out and one more coming back. A citizen who leaves the ring at full warmth, walks `d` cells out and returns, spends `2d` ticks in the cold and gets home on `100 - 2d`. The exchange rate is fixed and it says the colony's reach is fifty cells past the freezing line. That is the return motivation, and it wants no mechanism on top of it — only a citizen who can read it.

**What is missing is the lookahead, and that is what caution is.** ADR 0003 makes a threshold behaviour compare a level against a constant: warmth acts below 25 and stops at 75. Those marks are correct when the fire is a dozen cells away, because acting at 25 leaves 75 ticks of budget against a 13-tick walk. They are fatal at fifty, where acting at 25 leaves 75 ticks against a 50-tick walk, and the citizen turns for home on schedule, walks correctly, and dies 25 ticks short of the fire. The failure is not carelessness; a reactive controller with a fixed setpoint has no term for how far away its actuator is. Caution therefore enters as a comparison against the cost of getting home rather than against 25, and a per-citizen caution scalar is the margin they keep on top of that cost.

Conflicts with ADR 0003: the ADR fixes an enter and a leave threshold per need and the whole point of section 3's ruling was that the bands are display-independent constants. A warmth threshold that moves with distance from the fire is no longer a constant, and a citizen standing fifty cells out has a different low mark from one standing next to the generator. The hysteresis property the ADR exists to protect — that nobody oscillates on a doorstep — survives, because the gap between the marks is unchanged and only their position moves; but the ADR says constants and this says a function.

Ruling: adopt. The acting mark becomes the cost of getting home plus a margin, and the leaving mark moves with it, so the *gap* between the two — the thing hysteresis exists to protect — is unchanged. ADR 0003 is amended to say that a threshold is either a constant or a pure function of position, and that what the ADR guarantees is the gap rather than the value.

**Leave the patch when its marginal rate falls to the average including travel, from Charnov's marginal value theorem** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)). The theorem's own wording is that the forager should leave "when the marginal capture rate in the patch drops to the average capture rate for the habitat", where the average is taken over the whole cycle with travel counted as dead time. Its useful and counterintuitive consequence is that **longer travel means staying longer**, verified on Cowie's great tits by making the dish lids harder to open. Our adaptation is a substitution and has to be stated as one: the theorem's currency is energy and ours is warmth, because a hauler's patch time is not paid for in calories but in the body heat that a round trip costs. A citizen working a distant treeline should therefore strip it further before turning back than one working a near treeline, which is exactly the opposite of what a fixed give-up rule produces, and it is what makes a far patch worth having at all.

**Bigger loads from farther patches, from Orians and Pearson's central place foraging** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)). A forager that must carry a load back to a fixed base should carry more from farther away, because a fixed round-trip cost is amortised over the load; honeybees increase nectar load with travel time and beavers fell larger trees the farther they are from the lodge. Our adaptation: the colony already has half of this, since `food_yield(huts)` makes a haul of game worth more without changing the walk, and `haul_switch_margin` already sizes the deadband in hauls rather than units so the band scales with what a trip is worth. The missing half is that a trip's worth should also scale with how far it went. The evidence is a strong tendency and not a law — at least one study found no significant load-size-by-distance interaction — so this is a knob and not a certainty.

**A colony short of stores gambles; a citizen with warmth in hand does not, from the energy budget rule** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)). Caraco, Martindale and Whittam gave seven yellow-eyed juncos a constant perch that always covered the 24-hour energy cost against a variable perch of equal mean: on a positive budget the birds took the constant one, on a negative budget the variable one, and two birds retested individually **switched** when moved from surplus to deficit. Stephens formalised it as minimising the probability of falling short of a survival threshold, which is risk aversion above the bar and risk proneness below it. Our mapping is the interesting part, because the colony has **two** budgets and they disagree. The colony's budget is `stock_share` against the per-head target, and when it is negative the colony should accept longer, riskier trips because a safe short trip cannot close the gap. The citizen's budget is their own warmth, and when it is low they should turn back. So the risk term pushes a hauler out exactly as their own margin argues for coming in, and the citizen who dies is the one sent by a hungry colony on the day their own reserve was thin. That is the dramatic object this chapter exists to produce, and it falls out of applying one sourced rule at two scales. The caveat is large and must be carried: a 2024 critical review states that "the data do not match this prediction", with only 10 of 35 studies confirming the predicted switch, so this is adopted as a stylised rule and not as biology.

**A built heat bubble buys area at a price that rises faster than the radius, from Frostpunk's Steam Hub** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)). A hub costs 20 Steel and one road tile and burns 3 coal an hour at range 1 and 6 at range 2; Range Upgrade II costs 30 Wood and 20 Steel, extends range by 50%, which the wiki computes as 225% of the original heated **area**, and **doubles** the coal, with the same page conceding that nets "about +10% increase in efficiency". Our adaptation has to change the arithmetic, because our heat is not a radius band but a linear falloff: a source of output `h` reaches `h / HEAT_FALLOFF` cells, so radius is linear in output and area is quadratic in it, and the fuel a brazier burns is what buys output. The comparison the colony faces is then exact. A boiler adds `UPGRADE_HEAT` 18 to the ceiling at the centre, which is six more cells of ring for everyone; a brazier of the same output standing at a treeline forty cells out buys nothing at the centre and the whole forty for whoever is working there. Frostpunk's other rule comes with it: heat needs a connection, hubs are placed to consolidate rather than to save fuel, and the upgrade that looks like a bargain is not one.

**Caution is imprinted, not rolled, and the card is the channel** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md), [worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). Dwarf Fortress tracks a dwarf's eight strongest-emotion events each year, promotes them into permanent long-term memory, and lets those memories permanently shift facets and values; RimWorld is the negative case, where thoughts and traits are two systems that never convert. Our mapping: an expedition that killed someone is a colony event, colony events are already where cards are born (section 8's ruling), and the card the survivors carry raises their caution margin. Nothing new transmits. This is also what makes caution generational rather than personal — a card born of a bad winter is still shifting margins two decades later, which is the same clock section 15 uses for stats.

**A death out of sight is a missing citizen, from Dwarf Fortress** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)). DF gives a unit a **missing** status the instant it leaves the map, before any formal announcement; an unburied corpse gives unhappy thoughts to family and friends and can raise a vengeful ghost; and where the body cannot be recovered an engraved **memorial slab** calms the ghost without it, and reading the slab reports the dead unit's arrival and death or, failing that, the killer. Corpse hauling is a real labour and the wiki warns against giving it to depression-prone dwarves, because the hauler meets whatever killed the first one. Caves of Qud is the confirmed negative case: its death screen records cause of death and the last 30 log lines and there is no missing-person or memorial mechanic at all. Our mapping: the colony does not learn that a citizen died, only that they did not come back, and the chronicle line is a disappearance rather than a death. What turns it into a death is either a body recovered — which costs a second citizen a trip out to where the first one froze — or a slab raised without one, which closes the event at a cost in build capacity rather than in risk. Kenshi supplies the softer version if it is wanted: below a KO point of `10 + Toughness^0.75` a character enters a coma rather than dying, slavers pick up unconscious bodies, and a squadmate can carry an ally home ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)).

**The anti-source is RimWorld's caravans** ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)). Its numbers are fine — speed is `(13.6 * mount * mass ratio) / terrain difficulty` with the mass ratio running 200% empty to 50% loaded, travellers eat 1.6 nutrition a day, malnutrition accrues at 1.51-2.27% severity an hour through six stages and kills at 1.0 — and players still call packing "one of the most painful experiences in the game" and report that the cost of a run exceeds the reward. Half of that is interface friction, which a zero-player game cannot have. The other half is not: if an expedition's expected return does not beat staying home, the colony is right to refuse it and the whole layer is decoration. So the risk/reward gradient of section 13 is load-bearing for this section, and a run that never sends anyone past the near ring is a balance failure to be caught in the log, not a colony being sensible.

Open: how a citizen finds a patch nobody has visited. Option A keeps `gather_source` and lets the colony work only patches inside the radius the chunk loader has already realised, so exploration is a side effect of the map growing around the colony and nobody ever walks into the unknown deliberately. Option B gives an idle hauler a search walk, and the search-theory answer for sparse revisitable targets is a power-law step distribution with exponent about 2 ([worldgen §1b](research/worldgen.md)) — whose optimality is uncontested mathematics and whose status as a description of real animals was badly damaged when the albatross data behind it turned out to be a sensor artefact, then defended again on a million marine-predator records. Option B costs a deliberate scout role and a heavy-tailed draw from `noise`; option A costs the colony any story about someone who went looking.

Decision: option B, a deliberate scout walking a power-law step distribution with exponent about 2, drawn from seeded noise so a run still replays; the Lévy caveat stays cited beside it, because the mathematics is what is being adopted and the biology is not. Scouts are drawn from citizens the survival tier of the ballot has nothing to say about — being idle is the whole qualification. Option A dies on its own circularity: a chunk is realised because someone walked towards it, so a colony that only works realised chunks never walks anywhere new.

Open: what a waystation runs on. Option A gives it a fuel line, so a brazier only burns while somebody hauls to it and a chain of them is a standing tax on the workforce that grows with its length. Option B gives it a cache filled once, so it burns down on its own clock and a colony that forgets one has simply lost it, along with whoever was relying on it. Frostpunk's hubs are option A and are connection-gated; Don't Starve's campfires are option B and burn out in minutes.

Decision: option A, with a small on-site cache as the buffer. Haulers stock the cache, the brazier burns from it, and a chain of them is a standing tax on the workforce that grows with its length — which is the thing the colony should have to feel. Filling one once and forgetting it produces a silent loss with no system behind it, and a silent loss is the one failure a zero-player game cannot afford.

Open: whether caution is a scalar or a card. Option A gives each citizen a caution margin as a hidden stat in section 15's sense, formed and imprinted like the others, which keeps one representation for everything about a person. Option B makes caution purely the sum of the cards they carry, with no per-citizen number at all, which keeps the colony's whole psychology in one bitset and makes every cautious citizen cautious for a reason the watcher can name. Option B cannot express a citizen who is cautious for no reason; option A can, and has to explain why.

Decision: option B, cards only. There is one universal base margin, a constant, and everything above it comes from the cards a citizen carries, so every cautious citizen is cautious for a reason the watcher can read off their card. Unexplained individual variation is not abolished; it lives in section 15's residual, where it has a citation, rather than being duplicated here.

Open: whether the second tier of the ballot elects a waystation. `vote_of` already counts the hours a citizen spent walking back to the fire instead of working and answers every one of them with a generator upgrade, because a boiler is the only thing the colony can build that shortens that walk — and for a hauler working forty cells out the six cells a boiler adds are the wrong answer. Option A adds the waystation as a fourth building and lets the same detour count elect it, splitting the efficiency vote by where the detour was walked. Option B keeps the ballot at three buildings and sites waystations automatically wherever the detour count concentrates, which needs no vote and no new proposal type but takes a decision away from the citizens who pay for it.

Decision: option A. The waystation is a fourth building elected by the detour currency that already exists, split by where the detour was walked. The *site* may be computed from where those detours concentrate — that is arithmetic, not a decision — but the decision to build one belongs to the citizens who pay for it. Taking it away would make the colony's most spatial choice the only one nobody votes on, which is the thesis of the game inverted.

## 15. Stats a colony raises rather than rolls

Section 5 plans to roll strength, wits and hardiness on a fixed range at birth and print them as adjectives against the colony median. One roll of that family already ships: `colony_growth` gives every newborn `lifespan: lifespan_of(seed)`, a scatter of a tenth either side of `LIFESPAN_BASE`, and `hardiness(age, lifespan)` reads that number for the rest of the citizen's life, so a hash drawn at birth decides who winter takes forty years later. This section replaces the roll with a childhood, and it has to say what happens to the roll that already shipped.

The user intent is that stats pass culturally rather than genetically, and the constraint is that the colony gets no fourth transmission system: household and tradition cards are what exists, and the two of them have to carry it.

**Three transmission modes, and we already have two of them, from Cavalli-Sforza and Feldman** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). Their 1981 model names vertical transmission (parent to child), oblique transmission (a non-parental adult of the older generation to a naive individual) and horizontal transmission (peer to peer inside a generation), and the dynamics differ by pathway: vertical is the slowest channel there is and paces change at a generation, while horizontal is fast enough to replace an outdated behaviour inside one. They modelled the horizontal arm as an epidemic outright, which is where our SIR cards already sit. Our mapping: the household is the vertical channel and ADR 0009 already runs it, since a child takes the household trade with a fraction of the head's experience in it; the card is the horizontal channel and ADR 0006 already runs it. The oblique channel — the school, the trades standing around the child — is not a third mechanism but a card contact with an adult outside the child's own household, which is the whole of what this section needs to add.

**Biased transmission is how a card chooses its carriers, from Boyd and Richerson** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). Dual inheritance theory's taxonomy is content bias (the variant wins on its own merits), frequency-dependent bias (conformist copying of the majority more than in proportion to its frequency) and model-based bias, whose important case is prestige — deference freely conferred on a model worth copying, as against dominance, which is deference extracted. Our mapping: a card's contact rate is multiplied by the carrier's standing rather than being flat, which makes a mayor, an entertainer and a household head into transmission hubs without any of them getting a new mechanism. Conformist bias is the term that decides whether a colony converges or stays plural, and it belongs on the same season clock as the rest of culture.

**Skills compound and investment is a complement, not a substitute, from Cunha and Heckman** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). "The Technology of Skill Formation" names self-productivity — a skill acquired in one period persists and raises the productivity of the next period's investment — and dynamic complementarity — a unit of investment is worth more when the existing stock is already higher. Our mapping: the childhood accumulator is multiplicative rather than additive, so a warm well-fed early childhood makes a later good year worth more and a bad start cannot be bought back at full price. The Heckman curve itself is not adopted; it is contested in the literature and its author has since called it illustrative rather than estimated, so what is taken is the mechanism and not the shape.

**The formation input is already measured every tick.** `aging` computes `met_shares` over the whole population and feeds warmth and food into `maturation_rate`, which runs a child's clock between 0.6 and 1.6 years a year. The colony therefore already measures, on the hour clock, exactly the two quantities a childhood would be formed out of, and already spends them on how fast the child grows. Making them decide what the child grows into costs one accumulator on each citizen under `ADULT_AGE` and no new scan; the aggregate it reads is the one that is already there.

**Nutrition has a critical window and partial catch-up, from the Dutch Hunger Winter cohort** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). Women exposed to the 1944-45 famine before age 1 reached 4.45 cm less adult height and those exposed before age 2 4.08 cm less than unexposed controls, and the stunting window runs from about nine months before birth to twenty-four months after. But catch-up is real: height recovery frequently occurs between 24 months and adulthood even without supplementation, and adolescence is a second window, while cognitive effects of the early deficit persist more stubbornly than physical ones. Our mapping: the accumulator is weighted by age with the heaviest weight on the first years, hardiness and strength retain a catch-up term that a good adolescence can spend, and wits do not. That asymmetry is the whole reason the colony has two different stats to raise.

**Cold adaptation is exposure-driven and reverses, from the diving women of Korea and Japan** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). Their basal metabolic rate ran about 5% above standard in summer against 35% in winter, and the seasonal swing disappeared entirely once wetsuits cut the exposure after 1977. Our mapping: hardiness carries a slow acclimatisation term fed by hours actually spent in air below freezing and decaying when they stop, on top of whatever childhood set. This is the one honest way to have cold-hardened citizens without claiming the literature says a childhood locks hardiness in.

**The formation shape every shipped game converged on: exposure, sampled at milestones, durable afterwards** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). CK3 runs education from about 6 to 16 under a guardian whose own relevant skill plus half their Learning sets the tier, and picks childhood personality traits at discrete events around ages 3, 9, 11 and 13. RimWorld's Biotech gives growth moments at 7, 10 and 13 driven by a hidden growth score fed by the Learning need, and a child raised with that need frozen ends with zero passions, weak skills and three fully random traits. Workers & Resources prices it in buildings and years — kindergarten 0-4 to education level 0.5, school 5/6-17 for the 1.0 most jobs demand, university to a maximum of 3.0. Songs of Syx makes it a percentage where school reaches 60% and university closes the rest. Our mapping: the accumulator runs continuously but resolves at named milestones rather than every tick, which is both the shape the genre uses and the only version that fits the tempo budget.

**Keep a small random term, because the evidence says the unexplained part is large and unsystematic** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). Plomin and Daniels found most environmental variance in personality is nonshared, and Plomin's own 2024 update reports that thirty years of searching for its systematic causes mostly failed, with measured predictors explaining 1-2% of variance — the channel behaves like individual stochasticity, not an engineerable input. Turkheimer's third law says a substantial share of variance in complex behavioural traits is explained by neither genes nor shared family environment. Our mapping: `lifespan_of(seed)` survives as exactly that term. It stops being the thing that decides a citizen and becomes the residual on top of what the colony raised, which is what keeps two children of one house from being clones — the legibility requirement — with a citation rather than a hand-wave.

**Experience imprints personality, from Dwarf Fortress's memory system** ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)). DF tracks a dwarf's eight strongest-emotion events each year, promotes them into permanent long-term memory slots, and lets those memories permanently shift facets and values, desensitisation included. RimWorld is the negative case in the same sample: traits and thoughts are two systems that never convert into each other, so no amount of lived experience there mutates a trait. Our mapping: temperament and caution are imprinted by experienced events through tradition cards and nothing else, which is DF's pathway routed through the channel we already have. Section 14's expedition deaths and section 7's denied petitions are already card births; this is the same list read by a child.

Conflicts with section 5: that section rolls strength, wits and hardiness on a fixed range at birth, which is the mechanism this section replaces. What survives unchanged is everything downstream of the number — the bucketed adjective against the colony median, the converging estimate printed only after N observed worker-days, the two fit axes, and `effective_stat = base_stat * focus_mult * trade_fit_mult`. What changes is only where `base_stat` comes from, so section 5's ruling on hidden stats does not have to be reopened.

Ruling: the roll is replaced by childhood formation and everything downstream of `base_stat` is untouched. Section 5 keeps its text and gains a pointer here; ADR 0005 is amended on the origin of the number and on nothing else.

Conflicts with ADR 0005: the ADR says per-citizen stats are hidden and revealed through noisy performance. Nothing here contradicts that, but the ADR is silent on where the stat came from, and a stat the colony raised is one the colony could in principle have *watched* being raised — the warmth and food a child grew up in are colony-level quantities already on screen. Either the printed estimate stays gated on observed worker-days, or childhood conditions become a legitimate prior, and the two produce different citizen cards.

Ruling: childhood conditions are a legitimate coarse prior on the printed adjective, because the colony genuinely watched the upbringing and the warmth and food it happened in are already on screen. The estimate still converges through observed worker-days; the prior sets which band it starts in, never an exact value. A child of a fat decade therefore starts described as stronger than they may turn out to be, which is a better kind of wrong than starting from nothing.

Conflicts with ADR 0009: the ADR sets `lifespan` from a roll and derives frailty from it. Under this section the roll becomes a residual and the expected span becomes an output of childhood, which changes what the age pyramid means: a colony that ate well raises citizens who live longer, so the pyramid stops being only a maturation readout and becomes a compounding one.

Ruling: adopt. The roll becomes the residual, the expected span becomes an output of childhood plus the acclimatisation term, and the age pyramid compounds rather than merely reporting maturation. ADR 0009 is amended.

Conflicts with ADR 0006: the ADR's whole purpose is that eugenics-like policies are reachable only after generations of genuine cultural change, and the generational clock is card composition. Selection on upbringing has a much shorter feedback loop than selection on genome — a directed-upbringing policy shows its effect in one childhood, fifteen calendar years, rather than in the several generations a breeding programme needs. The gating machinery of ADR 0006 and ADR 0012 is untouched: the policy is still proposable only when a prerequisite card exceeds a carrier fraction and the harsh edge has crossed a threshold, and legitimacy still prices it. What changes is that once it passes, the colony sees the result inside a single run rather than beyond the horizon, which is a different dramatic object from the one the ADR describes. The survey found no shipped game that builds this arc on upbringing rather than genome, so there is no precedent to check the pacing against ([worldgen §1c](research/worldgen.md)).

Ruling: accept the shortened loop with eyes open. The gating machinery of ADR 0006 and ADR 0012 is unchanged — the policy is still proposable only on card composition and window position, and legitimacy still prices it — but the payoff horizon is now one childhood, fifteen game years, and the design record says so on purpose. That is the deliberate choice: the watcher sees the consequence in the children the colony raised, inside the run, rather than somewhere past its end. The survey found no shipped game that does this, so there is no precedent to check the pacing against and the balance log is the only instrument that will catch it going wrong.

Open: where the formation window closes. Option A ends it hard at `ADULT_AGE`, matching CK3's age-16 resolution and RimWorld's last growth moment at 13, which makes adulthood a clean readout and keeps the accumulator's lifetime bounded. Option B tapers it, keeping a diminishing adolescent catch-up term after 15, which is what the stunting literature actually reports and what makes a colony's recovery from a bad winter visible in the children who lived through it. The two differ in whether a colony can repair a cohort it half-starved.

Decision: option B, with the asymmetry the section already argues for made explicit. Hardiness and strength keep a diminishing adolescent catch-up term, so a colony can partly repair a cohort it half-starved; wits closes hard at `ADULT_AGE`, because that is the split the stunting literature actually reports — the physical deficit partly recovers and the cognitive one does not.

Open: what the milestones are. Option A resolves the whole accumulator once, at adulthood, so a citizen's stats appear when they join the workforce and the childhood is a single hidden integral. Option B resolves it in named steps like the genre's, at three or four ages, so a child visibly becomes something during the run and the chronicle has events to write. Option B costs more state and more chronicle variants; Option A costs the colony every story about a child.

Decision: option B, three or four named milestones. The chronicle needs children who visibly become someone, and a single hidden integral resolving at adulthood gives it nothing to write. The extra state and the extra chronicle variants are accepted as the price.

Open: how much of the outcome the random term should carry. The behaviour-genetics literature does not hand over a number that transfers — the variance components it reports are decompositions of real populations under conditions nothing like a freezing colony, and the Scarr-Rowe interaction says the split itself moves with resources, with shared environment explaining roughly 60% of IQ variance in impoverished families against near zero in affluent ones. Option A picks a flat residual share and states it as a legibility constant rather than an empirical one. Option B makes the residual share itself a function of colony prosperity, following Scarr-Rowe, so a comfortable colony produces citizens who differ for reasons it cannot see and a starving one produces citizens whose differences it can read straight off the granary.

Decision: option B, Scarr-Rowe, adopted as a stylised rule with its citation rather than as a transferred number.
